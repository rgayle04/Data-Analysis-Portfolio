#Methodology
  The Conservation Status Model (Model Trained from Animal Information Dataset) uses a Random Forest Algorithm. This machine learning model was selected due to the use of classification\prediction being the focus of this side of the project. The Random Forest Algorithm uses a series of decision trees to classify and separate data using the selected feature attributes. This allows it to classify our data into the three conservation status categories more effectively than other models that may require more binary values, such as the logistic regression model.

  The Global Temperature Prediction Model (trained on temperature and CO2 datasets) focuses on predicting rising global temperatures in recent years. Because of this, the model for this dataset will use a regression model to accurately predict temperatures, as they are continuous numerical values. The model chosen for testing is linear regression. Linear regression was selected because it can capture rising trends accurately. 

#Results 
  The Conservation Status Model was created utilizing a Random Forest Model. The model used several numerical and categorical columns for training, including Diet, Social Structure, Habitat 1 and Habitat 2, Countries Found, Gestation Period, Lifespan, and Average Speed. The model's target variable was Conservation Status, with three categories: Least Concern, Endangered, and Extinct. The model achieved 63% accuracy and a 63% F1-score in classifying the animal's conservation status. Notably, the model's most important features were lifespan, gestation period, average speed, habitat (Australia and wetlands), countries found (South America, Australia), and a solitary social structure. 
  
  This model used the CO2 and CO2 per capita columns as features to predict global temperatures. The line graph compares actual average temperatures with predicted temperatures over the years. This shows that the predictions are slightly off from the actual recorded temperatures, with some years spiking significantly and others dropping. However, the trend line mostly aligns with the recorded data. The Global Temperature Prediction Model achieved an R^2 of 0.578 with linear regression, indicating that while we have part of the picture, we need more information on how current trends form to better understand where we should be versus where we are. However, in 2023 and 2024, actual temperatures were notably higher than the model's predictions, indicating there is a significant rise. 

  When examining the relationship between Conservation Status and Rising Temperatures, we found a notable association. Species with a conservation Status of Endangered or Extinct tended to be more present in countries with higher average temperatures, while Least Concern species were more common in countries with much lower average temperatures. While this does not necessarily indicate causation or provide true evidence of a strong correlation between the rise of Endangered species and Rising Temperatures or Carbon Dioxide Emissions, this is an interesting finding and does indicate that the prior research done in Endangered and Threatened Species and Global Warming has a basis in its stance on the endangerment and extinction of species rising along with the temperature of the planet.
